Installing a Toilet in a Basement in Darien, IL

Yes, most basements can take a toilet. The real question is how the waste gets up and out, and that depends on where your sewer line sits.

Can you put a toilet in your basement?

Usually yes, but the answer depends on the height of your sewer line compared with the basement floor. If the main drain runs below the slab, waste can flow out by gravity. If the drain is at or above the floor level, waste has to be lifted with a pump.

That one fact, gravity or lift, decides the equipment, the digging and the work involved. A plumber checks the depth of your main line before recommending a setup. Illinois requires a license for plumbing work, and Darien requires permits when plumbing is changed.

The sections below explain each option, the vent rules and what to ask before you start.

Three ways to drain a basement toilet

Each setup suits a different basement. Choosing wrong is expensive to fix.

SetupHow it worksBest whenTrade-off
Standard gravity toiletWaste drops into a drain below the slabThe main sewer line is deeper than the floorNeeds the slab opened to run pipe
Sewage ejector pitA sealed pit with a pump lifts waste to the main drainThe drain is higher than the floor and you want full-size fixturesNeeds a pit cut into the slab and a sealed, vented lid
Upflush toilet with maceratorA unit behind or beside the toilet grinds waste and pumps it upYou cannot cut the slab or need a quick addMechanical parts, noise and limits on what can be flushed

A plumber should confirm the depth of the main drain and the pipe material before you decide.

How a basement toilet install usually goes

  1. 1

    Plan the location

    The plumber checks the main drain depth, the nearest vent and where framing allows a bathroom.

  2. 2

    Get the permit

    Ask Community Development about the permit and any inspection steps before work starts.

  3. 3

    Rough in the drain and vent

    Slab cutting, pit work or pump install happens, along with a vent line that reaches the roof or ties into an existing vent.

  4. 4

    Set the toilet and test

    The fixture is set, sealed and flushed repeatedly, and the pump or joints are checked.

Venting is not optional

Every toilet needs a vent so the drain flows freely and sewer gas stays out of the room. In a basement, the vent usually ties into an existing vent stack or runs up a wall to the roof. A vent that is too small or poorly placed causes slow flushing and gurgling.

Some upflush units use a small carbon filter vent and can be used where code allows. Ask the plumber and the City which approach applies. Never assume a studor-style air valve will satisfy a permit inspector without asking.

What Darien basements and older sewer lines mean for a new toilet

Darien began as the subdivisions of Marion Hills, Brookhaven, Farmingdale and Hinsbrook in the 1960s, and many homes there have basements with original sewer lines under the slab. Those lines were often cast iron or clay tile, and age can bring roots, offsets or cracks.

Adding a toilet puts more flow on that old pipe. Before you open the slab, a camera inspection can show whether the main line is in good shape. Our sewer camera inspection service gives you a video of the line. Fixing a collapsed pipe after you have finished a bathroom is a much bigger job.

Sump pump discharge is a separate system. The City asks residents to keep sump water off the sanitary sewer, so do not tie new basement plumbing into the sump line. Drain, vent and fixture work for the bathroom falls under the standards in the Illinois Plumbing Code (Title 77, Part 890 of the Illinois Administrative Code), and pump units come with manufacturer guidelines you should follow.

Your toilet will be the lowest fixture in the house

A basement toilet sits lower than every other fixture and often lower than the street sewer. If the sewer backs up, the lowest drain is where water appears first. Ask about a backwater valve or an alarm on the pit.

Also keep the toilet itself in good order. Our toilet repair and installation page explains how toilets are set, sealed and tested, which applies to basement units too.
