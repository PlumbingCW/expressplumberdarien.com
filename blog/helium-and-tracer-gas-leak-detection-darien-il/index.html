Helium and Tracer Gas Leak Detection: How Hidden Pipe Leaks Get Found

A tracer gas test fills a pipe with a harmless gas and listens for where it escapes. Here is how it works, what it is good at, and what it will not tell you.

What tracer gas detection is, in plain words

Tracer gas detection finds a leak by putting a light, harmless gas into an isolated pipe and then sniffing the surface above for any gas that escapes. Where the reading spikes, the pipe is leaking. Two gases are used most often: helium, and a blend of a small amount of hydrogen in nitrogen that is sold for exactly this purpose.

The idea is simple. Gas molecules are far smaller than water molecules, and they rise. A leak too small to stain a ceiling will still let gas through, and a sensitive handheld detector can pick up the trace at the floor, the drywall or the slab above. That lets a plumber mark a spot instead of guessing.

It is a locating method, not a repair. Once the spot is marked, the pipe still has to be opened and fixed. If you have a rising water bill or a wet floor and no visible source, call (630) 822-9290 and describe what you see. Our leak detection page explains the full process.

How a tracer gas test usually goes

Every home is different, so the order shifts a little. This is the general shape of the work.

  1. 1

    Confirm there is a leak

    The plumber shuts off fixtures and watches the water meter. A meter that keeps moving with everything off points to a leak somewhere between the meter and the faucets.

  2. 2

    Isolate the line

    The section under suspicion is closed off at both ends and drained, so gas can fill it without water in the way.

  3. 3

    Fill with tracer gas

    The gas goes in at low pressure through a fitting. It is mixed so that it is not flammable at the proportions used, and it has no smell or color.

  4. 4

    Scan the surface

    The plumber walks the floor and walls with a handheld detector, moving slowly and noting the highest readings.

  5. 5

    Mark and explain

    The strongest reading is marked. You get a plain explanation of what is under that spot and what opening the surface would involve.

Tracer gas compared with other ways to find a leak

No single method wins every time. A good plumber chooses by the pipe type, the floor finish and how the leak behaves.

MethodWhat it does wellWhere it struggles
Tracer gasFinds small leaks in pipes buried in slabs, walls and under finished floorsNeeds a pipe that can be isolated, and deep or very windy conditions weaken the reading
Acoustic listeningHears the hiss of pressurized water leakingTiny leaks are quiet, and traffic or HVAC noise drowns them
Thermal imagingShows cool or warm patches from leaking hot lines or wet materialsShows wetness, not the exact point, and works best with a temperature difference
Pressure testConfirms whether a line holds pressure at allSays yes or no, not where
Sewer cameraShows cracks, roots and offsets inside drain pipesDoes not help with pressurized supply leaks

Plumbers often combine two methods. Ask which ones fit your situation before you agree to work.

Why helium and hydrogen-nitrogen are the usual tracers

Helium is inert, light and not found in meaningful amounts in the air of a house, so a detector reading it means the gas came from the pipe. Its small atoms slip through tiny gaps quickly. It is more costly to buy than other options, which is one reason some crews prefer the blend.

The hydrogen-nitrogen mix is mostly nitrogen, the main gas in air, with a small share of hydrogen. At the proportions sold for leak work it is considered non-flammable. Hydrogen is the lightest gas there is, so it rises fast and a detector can respond to it at very low levels.

Neither gas is a gas-supply line fix. If you ever smell gas in your home, leave, then call 911 or your gas utility. Do not try to test or trace gas piping yourself.

What this means in Darien homes

Darien was incorporated in 1969 by residents of the Marion Hills, Brookhaven, Farmingdale and Hinsbrook subdivisions, so much of the housing dates from the 1960s onward. Houses from that era commonly have piping that was routed through slabs, crawlspace areas or finished walls, and some of those lines have now been in service for decades.

That is where tracer gas earns its place. If a line is running under a finished basement floor, breaking concrete in the wrong spot costs time and money and still may miss the leak. Marking the spot first limits how much of your floor has to be opened.

Darien buys treated Lake Michigan water from the City of Chicago, and it is metered. That makes the meter a useful first clue, since a dial that moves with every tap closed means water is going somewhere. The City's water operator number is (630) 353-8105 if you want to ask about your account.

It is not the first tool for every wet spot. These situations tend to justify it.

What tracer gas will not do

It will not tell you how bad the damage is, and it will not find a leak in a pipe that cannot be sealed off. It also will not work well if the pipe is filled with water, which is why the line is drained first.

Some leaks are in drain pipes, not supply pipes. For those, a camera is the better tool, and our sewer camera inspection shows cracks and offsets directly. If the water is coming from outside the house, the leak may be on the service line from the street, and a different repair applies.

Whatever repair follows a locate is done under the standards in the Illinois Plumbing Code (Title 77, Part 890 of the Illinois Administrative Code).
